# A Phoenician Journey

*Approved game vision · 10 November 2026*

## The vision

*A Phoenician Journey* is a 3D/2D educational game set on the ancient Phoenician coast at Byblos, in present-day Lebanon. The main character arrives at the coast and finds people at work. Players explore the harbor in 3D, approach artisans, and take part in focused 2D minigames that teach them how local goods were made.

The harbor and workshops tell one connected story: people make things, and those goods can travel through maritime trade. Players learn by helping with the work and seeing where its products go.

## What the player does

1. **Explore the 3D harbor.** Walk along the coast and discover workshops by seeing people at work.
2. **Approach an artisan.** Start a conversation and find out what they make and how you can help.
3. **Play a 2D workshop minigame.** Take part as an apprentice and work through a few representative steps in the production process.
4. **Return to the harbor.** Bring the finished product back into the world and see how it connects to trade.

Each craft sector is represented by a workshop and its own minigame. The 3D harbor gives players a place to discover and revisit those workshops; the 2D minigames give each craft room to show its materials and steps clearly.

## Purple dye workshop

Purple dye is the agreed example of how a workshop plays. The player sees people making dye, approaches the artisan, and is invited to help. In the 2D minigame, they learn how shellfish purple dye was made by working through a few selected steps and producing a piece of dyed cloth. Back in the harbor, a merchant asks for the cloth, showing how the work of an artisan connects to maritime exchange.

The project proposal cites evidence for shellfish purple dye at Sarepta. The game uses an imagined workshop at Byblos to teach the craft. A short illustrated note at the end of the activity explains what the evidence supports and what the game reconstructs.

## The Phoenician shipping letter

A sailor hands the player a letter that tells them **what to ship, how much, and where it is going**. At a scribe's table, the player reads a short excerpt from right to left and uses a reference card to match a few Phoenician signs to consonants. The scribe helps interpret the words so the player can act on the order. Matching signs teaches transliteration; understanding the instructions teaches translation.

At the docks, the player uses the letter to choose the goods and the destination, load the hold, and deliver the order to the right captain. Goods made in the workshops can become part of the shipment. If the player chooses the wrong cargo or ship, the dockworker points them back to the relevant line.

The first demo version uses **one order, two types of goods, and two destination choices**. An example order in English is: “From Byblos, send two bundles of purple cloth and three jars of wine to Sidon.” The goods, quantities, and destination in this example are illustrative.

The order is fictional. The [Saqqara papyrus](https://deaps.osu.edu/text_objects/11490) is a surviving Phoenician letter that discusses a payment and receipt, and the [Ahiram inscription](https://deaps.osu.edu/text_objects/11441) attests the name of Byblos as 𐤂𐤁𐤋 (GBL). Verify the game's Phoenician wording before using it.

## Learning goals

- Understand how a traditional production process turns materials into a finished good.
- Connect craft production to maritime exchange and recognize the Lebanese coast as a setting for Phoenician heritage.
- Read a few Phoenician signs from right to left and use a short written message to make a trade decision.
- Distinguish surviving historical evidence from interpretations and imagined details in the game.

## Team

Five people: two programmers, two designers, and one person who handles both 3D design and game design.

## Open decisions

The team has yet to decide which approved workshops to build for the prototype and which steps and mechanics to use in each one. It is also open whether the letter sends the player to workshops to gather goods or arrives after they have already made something. The character's identity and reason for arriving, the harbor layout and visual style, and the engine, target platform, and division of work are also open.
